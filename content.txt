1    CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION 
1.1 Introduction to Digital Privacy 
Almost everything people do today has some digital component to it. Talking to friends, 
paying for groceries, applying for a job, watching a show or checking the bank balance, all of 
this now happens through a phone or a computer rather than in person. Along the way, a huge 
amount of personal information passes through apps, websites and servers that most users 
never think about. Digital privacy is simply the idea that this information belongs to the 
person it describes, and that they should have some say in who gets to see it, use it or store it. 
It is easy to assume that privacy is something only "tech-savvy" people need to worry about, 
but that is not true. A person who has never written a line of code still has a phone number, a 
bank account, a set of photographs and a browsing history that can all be misused if they fall 
into the wrong hands. This project looks at how aware ordinary community members are of 
these issues and tries to build a case for why awareness matters. 
1.2 Background of the Study 
India has seen one of the fastest digital transitions in the world over the last several years. 
Cheap smartphones and inexpensive mobile data have brought millions of first-time users 
online, and services that used to require a physical visit, banking, bill payments, government 
forms, shopping, can now be completed from a phone in a matter of minutes. UPI-based 
payment apps in particular have changed how people transact, letting someone send money 
with just a phone number or a scanned code. 
This speed of adoption is impressive, but it has a downside. A lot of people started using 
digital services faster than they learned how to use them safely. Many users have never been 
taught what a phishing message looks like, why an OTP should never be shared, or why an 
app asking for access to contacts and location might be a red flag. Awareness has not always 
kept pace with adoption, and that gap is what this study is interested in exploring.      
2    1.3 Meaning and Concept of Digital Privacy 
At its core, digital privacy is about control, the ability to decide what personal information is 
shared, with whom, and for what purpose. It applies to many different kinds of data: 
identifying details like a name, phone number or email address; sensitive government 
identifiers such as an Aadhaar or PAN number; financial information like bank account or 
card details; login credentials such as passwords and PINs; one-time passwords (OTPs) used 
to verify transactions; and even less obvious data such as location history or app usage 
patterns. 
It helps to separate privacy from security, even though the two are related. Security is about 
the technical measures that keep data safe from unauthorised access, things like encryption or 
firewalls. Privacy is more about consent and control: whether a person actually knows what 
data is being collected about them and whether they agreed to it being used that way. A 
system can be technically secure and still violate someone's privacy if it collects more 
information than necessary or shares it without the user's knowledge. 
1.4 Importance of Digital Privacy in Everyday Life 
Digital privacy is not an abstract concern, it shows up in very ordinary situations. When 
someone logs into their banking app, buys something online, or messages a friend on social 
media, they are trusting that platform with a piece of their personal life. If that trust is 
misplaced, the results can range from minor annoyance, like spam messages, to serious harm, 
like a drained bank account or a stolen identity. 
This matters differently for different people. A student may mainly worry about social media 
privacy and account security. A working professional handling UPI payments and email for 
both personal and office use has more at stake financially. An older relative who has just 
started using a smartphone may be the most vulnerable of all, simply because they have had 
the least exposure to these risks. Digital privacy, in other words, is relevant to everyone who 
owns a phone, regardless of how comfortable they are with technology. 
1.5 Common Digital Privacy Risks 
There are a number of risks that come up repeatedly when people talk about digital safety:      
3    A. Weak passwords and PINs, reusing the same password everywhere, or choosing 
something easy to guess, makes it simple for an attacker to break into multiple 
accounts at once. 
B. Excessive app permissions, many apps ask for access to contacts, camera, 
microphone or location that they don't strictly need, and users often tap "allow" 
without a second thought. 
C. Phishing, fake emails, texts or calls that impersonate a bank, delivery company or 
government office to trick people into giving up sensitive details. 
D. Online payment fraud, fraudulent UPI requests, fake QR codes or scam calls asking 
for an OTP under the pretext of "verifying" an account. 
E. Hacked or compromised accounts, often the result of weak passwords, reused 
credentials, or clicking on malicious links. 
F. Data misuse, personal information being used for purposes the user never agreed to, 
such as unsolicited marketing or being sold to third parties. 
G. Oversharing on social media, posting personal details, travel plans or documents 
publicly, without realising how that information could be pieced together and 
misused. 
None of these risks are exotic or rare. Most people have either experienced one of them 
directly or know someone who has. 
1.6 Need for Digital Privacy Awareness in the Community 
Awareness is one of the cheapest and most effective defences against digital privacy risks, 
because a lot of these problems rely on the user themselves making a mistake, sharing an 
OTP, clicking a bad link, or ignoring an app permission. No amount of security technology 
can fully protect someone who is not aware of these basic precautions. 
This need is not evenly distributed either. Younger users may be comfortable with apps but 
careless about privacy settings, while older users may be careful in person but unfamiliar 
with how scams work online. A community-focused approach makes sense here, since people 
often learn safe habits from family members, neighbours and peers rather than from formal 
training. Raising awareness at the community level, even informally, has the potential to 
reach people who might otherwise never come across this information.      
4    1.7 Problem Statement 
Given how central digital services have become to daily life, and given the range of risks 
described above, there is a real need to understand how aware ordinary community members 
actually are of digital privacy issues, how they handle passwords and OTPs, whether they 
think about app permissions, how they protect personal information, and what experiences (if 
any) they have already had with privacy or fraud-related problems. This project addresses 
that gap by studying awareness levels within a specific community through a structured 
survey. 
1.8 Scope of the Project 
This project focuses on assessing digital privacy awareness among a defined group of 
community members using a survey conducted through Google Forms. A total of 105 
respondents took part in the main survey. The questionnaire was designed to cover several 
angles of digital privacy, general awareness, password and PIN protection habits, 
understanding of app permissions, protection of personal information, existing privacy 
practices, personal experiences with digital privacy issues, confidence in handling online 
risks, and opinions on the need for awareness programs. 
The project is limited to studying awareness and reported behaviour among this group of 
respondents; it does not attempt to build or test any technical security solution, nor does it 
aim to represent digital privacy awareness across the country as a whole. The detailed survey 
findings themselves are presented and analysed in the later chapters of this report, not here. 
1.9 Significance of the Study 
This study matters for a few reasons. It brings attention to a topic that affects nearly everyone 
but is rarely discussed directly, most people pick up digital habits informally, without ever 
really examining whether those habits are safe. By surveying a real community, the project 
can highlight where awareness is genuinely lacking rather than relying on assumptions. It 
also gives the researcher a chance to apply classroom knowledge of Information Technology 
to a real, socially relevant problem, which is the whole point of a Community Engagement 
Project. Finally, the findings from this study could serve as a small but useful starting point 
for anyone looking to design awareness efforts for a similar community in the future.      
5    CHAPTER 2: OBJECTIVES OF THE PROJECT 
2.1 Aim of the Project 
The main aim of this project is to understand digital privacy awareness, online safety habits 
and privacy-related experiences among community members through a structured survey 
conducted using Google Forms. Rather than assuming what people do or don't know about 
digital privacy, the project collects direct responses from a group of 105 participants to get a 
realistic picture of their habits, concerns and gaps in knowledge. The survey was designed to 
look at this from several angles, how respondents handle passwords and OTPs, whether they 
pay attention to app permissions, how carefully they share personal information, what privacy 
practices they already follow, what kinds of problems they have run into, how confident they 
feel about protecting themselves online, and whether they see a need for more awareness on 
this topic. 
2.2 Objectives of the Project 
Based on the questionnaire used in the survey, the specific objectives of this project are as 
follows: 
A. To understand the age groups of respondents and the devices they use to access 
the internet, in order to get a sense of who the respondents are and how they 
typically go online. 
B. To examine whether respondents use strong passwords and PINs, since weak or 
reused credentials are one of the most basic causes of account compromise. 
C. To understand whether respondents check app permissions before or after 
installing applications, as this reflects how much attention users pay to what data an 
app can access on their device. 
D. To study respondents' reported experiences of sharing sensitive personal 
information, such as OTPs, bank details or identity numbers, in order to see how 
often such information is disclosed and under what circumstances. 
E. To identify the digital privacy practices followed by respondents, including habits 
like using screen locks, adjusting social media privacy settings or reviewing account 
activity.      
6    F. To understand the digital privacy issues respondents have experienced or 
witnessed, such as phishing attempts, fraudulent calls or messages, or unauthorised 
use of personal information. 
G. To assess respondents' confidence in protecting their personal information 
online, which helps indicate whether people feel equipped to handle digital privacy 
risks or feel uncertain about it. 
H. To understand respondents' opinions about the need for digital privacy 
awareness programs, in order to see whether the community itself recognises this as 
an area worth addressing. 
I. To review the suggestions provided by respondents to improve digital privacy 
awareness in the community, so that any recommendations made later in this report 
can be grounded in what respondents themselves felt would help. 
Each of these objectives corresponds to a section of the survey questionnaire, so that the 
responses collected can be organised and discussed objective by objective in the later 
chapters of this report. 
2.3 Research Questions 
In line with the objectives above, this project seeks to answer the following questions: 
A. What age groups and devices are represented among the survey respondents? 
B. How common is the use of strong passwords and PINs among respondents? 
C. Do respondents check the permissions requested by the apps they use? 
D. How often, and in what situations, do respondents report sharing sensitive personal 
information? 
E. What digital privacy practices do respondents currently follow? 
F. What kinds of digital privacy issues have respondents experienced or witnessed? 
G. How confident are respondents in their ability to protect their personal information 
online? 
H. Do respondents feel that digital privacy awareness programs are needed in the 
community? 
I. What suggestions do respondents have for improving digital privacy awareness? 
      
7    CHAPTER 3: LITERATURE REVIEW / BACKGROUND 
STUDY 
3.1 Introduction 
Digital privacy and data security have emerged as critical areas of study alongside the rapid 
expansion of digital infrastructure and internet connectivity across the globe. As everyday 
services, communication, and financial transactions transition to online platforms, the volume 
of personal data generated and stored by third-party entities continues to grow exponentially. 
Understanding the existing body of knowledge regarding digital privacy requires examining 
foundational concepts, technical safeguards, regulatory frameworks, and user awareness 
patterns. This chapter reviews established literature on digital privacy, personal data 
protection, authentication methods, mobile application behaviors, online threats, and the legal 
landscape in India. 
3.2 Concept of Digital Privacy 
Digital privacy broadly refers to an individual's right and ability to control their personal 
information, determining when, how, and to what extent such data is communicated to others. 
Academic and institutional discussions often distinguish between data privacy and 
information security. While information security focuses on technical mechanisms—such as 
encryption, firewalls, and secure servers—designed to prevent unauthorized access, data 
privacy centers on governance, policy, consent, and user autonomy. 
 
Scholars note that modern digital ecosystems heavily rely on commercial business models 
often described as surveillance capitalism. In these frameworks, platforms track user 
behavior, search histories, location patterns, and social interactions to curate personalized 
content and targeted advertisements. This dynamic frequently creates an imbalance between 
institutional data collection practices and user awareness, making the preservation of 
individual privacy an ongoing challenge in modern society. 
3.3 Personal Data and Data Protection 
Personal data encompasses any information relating to an identified or identifiable living 
individual, ranging from basic identifiers like names and contact numbers to sensitive      
8    financial records, biometric markers, and government-issued identification numbers. 
Effective data protection requires adherence to core principles such as data minimization, 
purpose limitation, transparency, and accountability. 
 
International guidelines and institutional frameworks emphasize that organizations collecting 
user data must secure explicit consent and provide transparent disclosures regarding data 
processing activities. Protecting personal data is essential not only to prevent financial loss 
and identity theft but also to uphold fundamental human dignity and autonomy in an 
increasingly digitized world. 
3.4 Digital Privacy Awareness Among Internet Users 
Research consistently indicates a significant gap between rapid technological adoption and 
user awareness of digital risks. While broad segments of the population utilize smartphones 
and digital services for daily tasks, many lack formal training in cybersecurity principles. 
 
Studies examining user behavior highlight that awareness levels vary across different 
demographic groups. Younger demographics often display higher familiarity with social 
media privacy settings and application interfaces, yet they may still engage in risky behaviors 
such as oversharing personal details or reusing credentials across multiple platforms. 
Conversely, older adults or first-time digital users frequently encounter steep learning curves, 
making them particularly susceptible to social engineering and online fraud due to limited 
exposure to digital threats. 
3.5 Password Security and Authentication 
Authentication mechanisms, particularly passwords and personal identification numbers 
(PINs), serve as the primary line of defense against unauthorized account access. Academic 
literature and security advisories from agencies like the Computer Emergency Response 
Team India (CERT-In) emphasize that weak, predictable, or reused passwords severely 
compromise account security. 
 
Despite widespread recommendations to implement complex, unique passwords paired with 
multi-factor authentication (MFA) or one-time passwords (OTPs), user habit studies show a 
persistent reliance on convenience over security. Common pitfalls include using birthdates,      
9    simple sequential numbers, or identical login credentials across multiple websites and 
applications, which allows a single data breach to cascade across multiple personal accounts. 
3.6 Mobile Application Permissions and Data Privacy 
Mobile applications frequently request access to core device features and hardware, including 
contacts, location services, cameras, microphones, and storage. While many permissions are 
necessary for specific app functionalities, excessive or blanket permission requests pose 
latent privacy risks. 
 
Behavioral studies regarding mobile applications demonstrate that users frequently grant 
permissions blindly during installation without reviewing privacy policies or evaluating 
whether the requested access is proportional to the app's actual utility. This lack of scrutiny 
enables applications to harvest extensive background data, location histories, and contact 
directories without explicit, informed ongoing consent from the user. 
3.7 Phishing, Online Scams and Protection of Personal Information 
Social engineering attacks, most notably phishing, remain prevalent vectors for cyber fraud. 
Phishing involves deceptive communications—such as fraudulent emails, text messages, or 
phone calls—impersonating trusted entities like banks, delivery services, or government 
agencies to trick individuals into disclosing sensitive credentials, banking details, or OTPs. 
 
Security frameworks stress that technical filters alone cannot eliminate phishing risks because 
these attacks exploit human psychology and trust. Consequently, cultivating skepticism 
toward unsolicited communications, verifying sender identities, and exercising caution when 
sharing personal information online are highlighted as critical protective habits for all internet 
users. 
3.8 Digital Privacy in the Indian Context 
India's digital landscape has expanded rapidly, propelled by affordable mobile data, extensive 
smartphone penetration, and unified payment architectures like the Unified Payments 
Interface (UPI). However, this rapid transition has also exposed millions of users to emerging 
cyber threats. 
      
10    From a regulatory standpoint, the legal framework governing privacy has evolved 
significantly. A landmark milestone was established by the Supreme Court of India in the 
2017 judgment of Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) vs. Union of India, which explicitly 
recognized the right to privacy as a fundamental right under Article 21 of the Constitution. 
Subsequently, the Indian Parliament enacted the Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) 
Act, 2023, establishing a comprehensive statutory framework for processing digital personal 
data, outlining obligations for data fiduciaries, and defining the rights and duties of digital 
citizens. Official guidance from the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology 
(MeitY) and CERT-In continues to stress public awareness, secure digital hygiene, and 
prompt reporting of cyber incidents. 
3.9 Review of Relevant Studies 
1. Saini et al. (2021) evaluated the efficacy of cybersecurity measures and education 
frameworks in India, concluding that institutional mechanisms alone are insufficient 
without active community-level education and behavioral shifts toward digital safety. 
 
2. Research by policy initiatives analyzing young adults' online privacy (2023) 
investigated user awareness regarding social media data harvesting. The findings 
revealed that while a majority of young users recognized basic profile visibility 
settings, significantly fewer understood deeper commercial data monetization 
practices, highlighting a nuanced gap in technical literacy. 
 
3. Government and institutional reviews (CAG, 2024) have underscored that rapid 
digitization of public and financial services must be paired with robust awareness 
campaigns to protect vulnerable citizens from identity theft, payment frauds, and 
unauthorized data exposure. 
3.10 Research Gap 
While existing literature extensively examines national cybersecurity policies, 
macroeconomic data protection frameworks, and large-scale enterprise compliance, there 
remains a need to understand how ordinary community members perceive and navigate 
digital privacy in their everyday routines. Macro-level studies often overlook micro-level      
11    practical habits—such as individual password management, app permission checks, and 
immediate responses to online fraud. 
 
This project addresses this localized gap by conducting a structured community survey 
among 105 respondents. Rather than evaluating technical network security or formulating 
national policy, this study captures self-reported awareness levels, daily digital habits, and 
practical challenges faced by community members, thereby providing grounded insights into 
grassroots digital privacy literacy. 
3.11 Summary 
This chapter has examined the multifaceted nature of digital privacy, tracing its evolution 
from foundational data protection concepts to technical authentication safeguards, mobile 
application behaviors, and social engineering risks. Furthermore, it outlined the Indian 
regulatory environment, highlighting the significance of the Digital Personal Data Protection 
Act, 2023, and landmark judicial pronouncements. By reviewing relevant scholarly works 
and identifying the gap concerning community-level awareness, this background establishes a 
solid foundation for analyzing the primary survey data presented in the subsequent chapters 
of this project. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
      
12    CHAPTER 4: METHODOLOGY 
4.1 Project Design 
This project adopts a survey-based descriptive research design to examine digital privacy 
awareness, online safety habits, and related experiences among community members. A 
survey-based approach was selected because it allows for the systematic collection of 
quantitative and qualitative data directly from participants regarding their everyday 
behaviors, perceptions, and knowledge gaps. A structured questionnaire serves as a suitable 
instrument for this purpose, as it ensures that every respondent answers the same set of 
standardized questions, thereby enabling consistent measurement and comparison across 
responses. The project utilizes descriptive analysis of the collected survey data, organizing 
responses to present a clear picture of prevailing awareness levels and practices within the 
target group without attempting to manipulate variables or test a formal experimental 
hypothesis. 
4.2 Target Community / Beneficiaries 
The intended participants for this study comprise community members across different age 
groups]. The survey successfully gathered 105 responses from participants representing 
diverse demographic backgrounds. These respondents act as the primary population for 
evaluating current digital habits related to password security, app permissions, and personal 
data sharing. The potential beneficiaries of this study include community members 
themselves, who can gain indirect awareness through reflection on the surveyed issues, as 
well as future student researchers or local organizers seeking baseline insights to design 
targeted digital literacy and privacy awareness initiatives. 
4.3 Data Collection Methods 
Data collection was executed using Google Forms, a cloud-based survey management tool 
that allows questionnaires to be distributed digitally. The questionnaire was structured to 
address multiple dimensions of digital privacy through a total of nine main questions. The 
instrument incorporated various question formats, including multiple-choice questions for 
categorical variables, a multiple-select question to capture overlapping behaviors, a rating-
scale question to assess perceived confidence levels, and an optional open-ended suggestions 
question to invite qualitative feedback from participants. The optional open-ended      
13    suggestions question successfully received 51 responses. The gathered data is summarized 
and analyzed using quantitative counts, percentages, structured tables, visual charts, and 
thematic grouping of the written qualitative feedback. 
4.4 Activities Conducted 
The execution of the project followed a systematic workflow consisting exclusively of 
confirmed, completed activities: 
 
A. Topic Selection and Scope Definition: Identifying digital privacy awareness within 
the community as the core subject of study and outlining the project objectives. 
B. Questionnaire Preparation: Drafting the structured questionnaire items covering 
demographic details, password hygiene, app permissions, privacy practices, and 
personal experiences, and implementing them into an online form. 
C. Form Distribution and Response Collection: Distributing the survey link to 
potential respondents within the community and collecting a total of 105 completed 
responses. 
D. Data Organization and Analysis: Compiling and organizing the recorded responses 
into structured formats to evaluate trends, calculate frequencies, and synthesize 
qualitative suggestions. 
4.5 Resources and Tools Used 
The completion of this project relied on a specific set of digital resources and productivity 
tools: 
 
A. Google Forms: Utilized for creating, hosting, and administering the online 
questionnaire and collecting digital responses. 
B. Google Sheets / Spreadsheet Tools: Used for organizing, sorting, and managing the 
raw response dataset. 
C. Hardware Devices: A computer and smartphone utilized for browsing, form creation, 
data monitoring, and report drafting. 
D. Internet Access: Required for online form hosting, literature referencing, and 
continuous connectivity.

5.1 Data Presentation 
The process of data analysis is essential for transforming raw survey responses into 
meaningful insights. In this project, a structured survey instrument administered online was 
utilized to gather information regarding digital privacy awareness, online safety habits, and 
privacy-related experiences among community members. Analyzing these responses helps us 
understand how everyday users navigate digital platforms, protect their personal information, 
and perceive online security risks.

surveyed group.

comments and recommendations, which were subsequently analyzed using thematic

activate secondary verification methods (such as SMS codes, authenticator apps, or 
biometric locks) across all critical email, financial, and social media accounts.

inspect app permissions only occasionally or never, individuals should review app 
access rights before and after installation. Restricting unnecessary access to device      
37    microphones, cameras, contact lists, and location services helps prevent unwarranted 
background data harvesting.

acknowledging the sharing of sensitive details like OTPs or banking information, 
individuals must exercise extreme caution. Clear personal rules should be established 
never to disclose OTPs, PINs, Aadhaar numbers, or bank credentials over phone calls, 
text messages, or unverified web links. 
 
● Maintain Regular Device Updates: Users should routinely update their 
smartphones, computers, and installed applications to patch known security 
vulnerabilities and protect against emerging malware threats.

● What the project accomplished: The project successfully developed a structured 
survey instrument, collected 105 responses, and documented participating community 
members' reported privacy practices, experienced issues, confidence levels, and      
44    opinions. This data provided a clear empirical snapshot of community concerns and 
awareness needs. 
 
● What the findings helped identify: The analysis pinpointed specific areas where 
respondents excel, such as password creation and 2FA adoption, alongside areas 
needing improvement, such as app permission checks. Furthermore, the optional 
suggestions offered valuable perspectives on how future educational content can be 
delivered effectively. 
 
● What cannot be claimed: No separate educational intervention, training workshop, 
or before-and-after assessment was conducted as part of this project. Consequently, 
the project did not directly increase participants' awareness, change their online 
behavior, prevent fraud, or reduce security risks. The observed impact is strictly 
limited to the collection, organization, and interpretation of community feedback, 
rather than a measured transformation in participants' knowledge or conduct. 
8.6 Overall Assessment of Project Impact 
In summary, this community engagement project played a meaningful role in documenting 
the digital privacy practices, concerns, and preferences reported by 105 participating 
respondents. By exploring device usage, credential habits, permission scrutiny, and exposure 
to phishing and online scams, the project established a grounded understanding of grassroots 
digital awareness. 
 
While the survey reflects the views and behaviors of its specific participants rather than the 
entire population, the overwhelmingly positive community response toward awareness 
programs—evidenced by 64 Yes and 32 Maybe votes—highlights a clear readiness for future 
educational initiatives. Ultimately, the project's impact lies in its ability to identify key areas 
of vulnerability and community interest, providing a reliable foundation for educators, 
students, and local organizers planning future digital safety campaigns. 
 
      
45    CHAPTER 9: LIMITATIONS OF THE PROJECT 
9.1 Introduction 
The project, titled "Digital Privacy Awareness for Community", was conducted to understand 
the digital privacy practices, online safety experiences and awareness-related opinions of 
community members. Information was collected through a Google Forms questionnaire 
containing nine main questions and an optional question for suggestions. While the survey 
provided useful information about the participants, certain limitations must be considered 
when interpreting the findings. These limitations mainly relate to the scope of the responses, 
the use of self-reported information and the extent to which the questionnaire could assess 
actual digital privacy practices. 
9.2 Sample Size Limitations 
The survey received 105 responses to its nine main questions. These responses provided 
information about the participants' digital privacy practices and experiences. However, the 
number of responses alone does not establish whether the sample represents the wider 
community. The available information does not establish that respondents were selected 
through a random sampling process or that the sample was designed to represent a particular 
population. 
Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as the responses of the individuals who 
participated in the survey. They should not automatically be treated as representative of all 
community members. The limitation concerns the extent to which the findings can be 
generalized, rather than the number of responses being inherently insufficient. 
9.3 Geographical and Population Limitations 
The geographical coverage of the survey and the exact population from which respondents 
were recruited are not established in the available project information. As a result, it is not 
possible to determine how closely the participants represent a particular locality or a wider 
population.      
46    Although the survey included respondents from different age groups, the distribution was not 
equal. The largest group consisted of participants aged 18 to 25. Consequently, the findings 
may reflect the experiences and opinions of the participating age groups differently. 
The results should therefore be understood within the scope of the respondents rather than 
generalized to an entire town, state or country. 
9.4 Self-Reported Response Limitations 
The questionnaire relied on information provided by the respondents themselves. Participants 
reported their password practices, app permission habits, experiences with digital privacy 
issues and confidence in protecting personal information. 
Such responses reflect the participants' understanding and recollection of their own 
experiences. Some respondents may have interpreted a question differently, forgotten 
particular incidents or selected an answer that they considered socially acceptable. These are 
possible limitations of self-reported surveys and cannot be confirmed for individual 
responses. 
The survey did not independently verify the accuracy of each participant's answers. 
Therefore, the results should be interpreted as reported practices and experiences rather than 
independently confirmed facts. 
9.5 Questionnaire Limitations 
The questionnaire covered selected aspects of digital privacy through nine main questions. 
Although these questions provided information about several important topics, they could not 
explore every aspect of digital privacy in detail. 
The closed-ended questions offered predefined response options, which made responses 
easier to compare but limited the opportunity for participants to explain their experiences in 
their own words. The optional suggestions question allowed respondents to express additional 
views. However, only 51 participants submitted a response to this question, and some 
answers were brief or did not contain a specific suggestion.      
47    Consequently, the optional responses provided additional perspectives but did not capture 
detailed feedback from every participant. 
9.6 Time and Resource Constraints 
The available project information does not establish specific time-related or resource-related 
difficulties encountered during the survey. Therefore, no particular constraint can be 
identified as a confirmed limitation of this project. 
The study was limited to the information collected through the questionnaire. Additional 
research methods, such as detailed interviews or follow-up assessments, could provide further 
information, but their absence should not be treated as evidence of a specific time or resource 
problem. 
9.7 Limitations in Measuring Actual Privacy Behavior 
The survey collected participants' reports about practices such as using strong passwords, 
enabling two-factor authentication, checking app permissions and avoiding the disclosure of 
sensitive information. However, these practices were not independently examined or 
technically verified. 
For example, selecting that one uses a strong password does not establish the actual strength 
or uniqueness of that password. Similarly, reporting that app permissions are checked does 
not confirm how consistently this practice is followed. 
The questionnaire also did not include a before-and-after assessment of an educational 
intervention. Therefore, it cannot establish whether participants' knowledge, awareness or 
behavior changed as a result of the project. The findings describe reported practices and 
opinions at the time of the survey. 
9.8 Summary 
The main limitations of the project relate to the representativeness of the respondents, the 
unspecified geographical coverage, the reliance on self-reported information and the scope of 
the questionnaire. The survey provided information about the digital privacy practices and      
48    experiences reported by its participants, but it did not independently verify their behavior or 
measure changes in awareness. 
These limitations do not invalidate the collected responses. They clarify the boundaries 
within which the findings should be interpreted and help ensure that conclusions remain 
based on the information actually collected.
